The Gulmohar Series

01 Short fiction Mrs Crawford’s Daily Musings A crow observes the humans beneath her Gulmohar tree. Read

Hello Dear I am Mrs. Crawford — mother, orator, creator, nature lover, and an eloquent speaker, among many other things. But to humans, I am merely a cawing crow. A gross underestimation, dare I say.

I sit in a lovely spot on this 100-year-old property, a place my ancestors took immense pride in — the grand, ever-blooming Gulmohar tree. The elders in our Friday commune, perched atop this very abode, often retell the tale of how my great-great-grandparents, with remarkable foresight, chose this spot to drop the seeds of what is now the Mighty Gulmohar.

They say the seeds were a gift, fetched by our cousins who flew in from the West for the Annual Migratory Flying Olympics. A once-honorable tradition, now tainted by corruption and favoritism. The family hardly cares to visit anymore, and frankly, the Olympics have become a sham these days. We were shooed away once, and ever since, we have refrained from even being spectators to their disgraceful spectacle.

And yet, it’s hard to ignore. Especially when humans have taken a fancy to it, blatantly copying our ancient tradition of soaring through the skies — though their pathetic, wingless attempts at flight have been reduced to the ridiculous spectacle they call the ‘high jump.’ A bastardization of our age old craft.

But I digress. I am a simple Crawford lady, elegant, sharp, and with a face card that refuses to be anything but iconic. But like any member of my lineage, I carry a duty. A duty to speak for my kind about hunger, climate change, political and social injustice. And yes, occasionally, I indulge in a little gossip and a well-earned rant.

But above all, I observe. And I have observed you. Day in and day out, through storms and sunshine, in your quiet moments and your loudest chaos. I have seen your rise, your fall, your endless cycles of greed and generosity, destruction and repair, love and war. I have watched you build, destroy, and rebuild, all while pretending the past never existed. You believe yourselves to be masters of the world, yet you stumble through it like blind fledglings from a broken nest.

By now, you must already be aware because my eyes are but on you, humans. And I have much to say.

02 Short fiction A Regret Over Gulmohar A tree, a cyclone, and the grief of not having looked closely enough. Read

It was one of those great summer mornings. I was on my usual routine — driving to my office. I have always liked summer season albeit the heat wreaks havoc on poor plants and thirsty crows. But there was one tree I used to come across everyday while driving. Under the filter of my tinted glasses, the huge Gulmohar seemed purposefully intense. The orange and green hues popped, competing for a striking contrast with the blue sky. Call it my ignorance but, I always thought that the rear windows of my vehicle captured the Gulmohar way better than any camera could.

Few days after the patient admiration of the Gulmohar tree, I was inside closed doors and windows of my home, trying to escape from the massive winds which brought with itself destruction, devastation and heartbreaking stories. It was unusual for cyclones to batter this corner of the world at this month of the year.

The following seven nights after Fani — the super cyclone — were like taking a tour to the dark ages without a flash light. Thousands were dragged to homelessness, starvation and the other hundreds to death. Havoc prevailed. The city’s coconut trees were slapped hard by the winds that changed its direction twice! Funny how Fani laughed off the beautification that was carried out during the hockey world cup in 2018. It ripped apart each and every poster in the city like a cat on heat. It was indifferent to the political parties to which the ad. posters belonged to.

After a noisy night, came a much awaited daybreak. 24 hours later, I slid the glass windows of my room. Light came in along with mild breeze. It was difficult to find anything green. A sad silence was all I could hear. As I opened the door, something damp embraced on the tip of my right toe. Some jobs in the world require a level of dedication that an email sent on a weekend can hardly match. I thought as I picked up the soaking newspaper. Emboldened headlines and pictures of fallen trees I saw. My soul lacked any enthusiasm to go through it. I needed water.

By evening, the breeze had moved past. People were coming out and measuring their losses. Adults grieved about their broken antennas whilst children worried about their treasured pencil-box that lay under the debris of shattered roofs and fallen debris. A few broken asbestos carried from neighboring buildings lay on streets without any entitlement. Amongst the heap of losses, I brought my scooter out onto the road and went on a ride to map out the wrath of the intense winds.

For a moment I forgot if there ever was a concrete road because all my eyes were able to see were once majestic trees lying lifelessly on the ground. The proudest ones were destined for the worst. I wondered “Do you know what must happened to the Gulmohar?” I wanted to know. All the while, I regretted taking a snap or admiring its luscious petals a little more. May be spend some time under its shadow whilst the flowers fall off one by one.

As i took sharp turns and awkward ways, I reached a place hardly known to me. It was the same route I took for office. Confused, I looked around and then desperately towards the leaf covered road. As if something called me from beneath, I found the orange tint again. The Gulmohar had fallen.

03PoemAestheticAn ode to the ironies of Mumbai.Read

There’s Something aesthetic
in the noise of a local — in the array of lights
blocking the violet sunset
as it blushes pink, as my eyes descend
from the fiftieth floor
to the child below,
peeing across the railway track.

There’s something aesthetic
in slyly glimpsing
the dangerous romance
of lovers against the window-grills, beside the open doorway
of the roaring local.

Something more aesthetic
in the thought of poverty — my eyes trailing
across dirty shacks,
drooping roofs,
yellow-tinted walls — until I realise: this is not where
civilisation began,
but where it is going
to end.

The thought is struck from me
as my hair lifts
in the wake of the local
rushing towards Kalyan. Mumbai — aren’t you aesthetic
from up here?

04PoemRooms, Cities, MonsoonsA letter to a best friend and roommate leaving a crammed Jamia PG for Dublin.Read

I looked at loneliness closely
alone in the room, the city, during monsoon. I blame you for this feeling
because you made me feel at home.

Now you’re leaving the room, the city, during monsoon. My friend, don’t pack away all the memories.
Leave some behind for me,
for I’ll miss our tea and samosas in our room, our city, during monsoon.

Time passes so differently for each of us; it’s strange.
It was just yesterday at midnight when we got dazed and drunk. Tomorrow, yours will be a new room, a new city,
and mine will remain this monsoon.

I wonder if time zones will affect our friendship.
Maybe they will, maybe they won’t… But if, like me, you look at loneliness closely someday,
remember: I’m there no matter the different rooms, different cities,
or different monsoons.

05PoemमीमA small note about the last ember of a fading connection.Read

सुनो ज़रा, क्या हाल है आजकल? दिखाई नहीं देते, सुनाई नहीं देते, सिर्फ़ भूत की तरह अपने होने का एहसास देते हो, हर घंटे, हर पल, एक मीम भेजकर।

हाँ, हाँ, बहुत अच्छा sense of humour है तुम्हारा। ठीक है, तुमने ये jokes लिखे नहीं हैं, पर चलो, share तो कर रहे हो न। देख लिया हमने। हँसी आए या न आए, “Ha ha” emoji से react कर दिया है।

पर सच बताऊँ, बुरा नहीं मानोगे? मैंने आजकल तुम्हारे jokes देखना बंद कर दिया है। बस like कर देती हूँ, ताकि तुम्हारे दिल को ठेस न पहुँचे और तुम मुझे memes भेजना बंद न कर दो।

क्योंकि अब यही आख़िरी लौ बची है हमारे बुझते हुए सम्पर्क की, और मुझे इसे जलाए रखना है।
